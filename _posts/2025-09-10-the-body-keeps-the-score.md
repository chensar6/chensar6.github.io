**TW: Rape, Murder, War**

One thing I will never fall for is the propaganda AGAINST annotating books. I've run into people who think that writing or marking directly on a book ruins it... But when you have a book collection in your 50s and you're revisiting old books you've read, would you not want to see books that have been well-loved and well-read? Do you not want to see the sections that you loved and the thoughts you had in the past?

I first discovered the joys of annotating books when I read through a book that was annotated by a friend. I found myself enjoying the book more. It was exciting to run into the next passage that would be underlined and to be able to see what parts mattered to my friend. And from then, I began annotating my books. 

I've been reading this book: *The Body Keeps The Score*, written by Bessel Van Der Kolk, M.D. I do want to preface that at this time of writing this post, I have not finished the book and am halfway through. When I finish, I hope to give an update on this blog.

Now *The Body Keeps The Score* is a little controversial, but I understand why. He was born in 1943 and the start of his PTSD research really started in the 1970s. This book is a work of its time. From my understanding, PTSD research really got off the ground due to veterans, and a good chunk of the anecdotes he shares are with the veterans he worked with at the VA. It's very nuanced, on one end, I find myself utterly appalled at what these veterans did to innocent women and children, while getting return to America, alive (but now with PTSD). 

On the other end, I am a strong believer of a society that rehabilitates. I think that both of these can co-exist, and I've run into reviews of people not finishing the book because the author gives a platform to people who, for instance, raped women and killed children in Vietnam, in a sense, humanizing these veterans and trying to heal them. I understand this anger, especially if your lineage comes from these countries that were in opposition to America during this time. Healing these veterens can almost feels like a get-out-of-jail-free card but for the guilt. I believe that they should remember every horrific thing that they did, but a healthy society should, in my eyes, have an emphasis on rehabilitation, which doesn't mean they get to forget about it. I'm not completely educated on this topic, but this was all to say, that the diagnosis and research for PTSD was significantly influenced by the experience of veterans and that if talking about PTSD (which is what this book is about), veterans are going to be a big part of it.

One thing I would have really enjoyed is more depth in the social sciences, but also, he's an M.D. (not a social scientitist) from the 1970s. I think that's an explanation as to why there isn't much depth into intersectionalty, e.g. race, socioeconomic statuses, education, immigration, etc., and how these factors influence trauma and vice versa. But perhaps this topic is meant for a entirely separate book. Though for such a well-known book that is often raved about, I would have hoped for more.

Besides all of this, I do think that there are insights to be gained. I wanted to share some passages:

> Sacred animals return home, regardless of whether home is safe or frightening. I thought about my patients with abusive families who kept going back to be hurt again. Are traumatized people condemned to seek refuge in what is familiar? If so, why, and is it possible to help them become attached to places and activities that are safe and pleasurable? (p.31)

> Desensitization may make you less reactive, but if you cannot feel satisfaction in ordinary everything things like taking a walk, cooking a meal, or playing with your kids, life will pass you by. (p.73)

> If doctors can't agree on what ails their patients, there is no way they can provide proper treatment. When there's no relationship between diagnosis and cure, a mislabeled patient is bound to be a mistreated patient. You would not want to have your appendix removed when you are suffering from a kidney stone, and you would not want to have somebody labeled as "oppositional" when, in fact, his behavior is rooted in an attempt to protect himself against real danger. (p.167)

> Writing experiments from around the world, with grade school students, nursing home residents, medical students, maximum-security prisoners, arthritis sufferers, new mothers, and rape victims, consiistently show that writing about upsetting events improves physical and mental health. (p.243)

> A different problem arises when trauma victims themselves become literally speechless - when the language area of the brain shuts down. I have seen this shutdown in the courtroom in many immigration cases and also in a case brought against a perpetrator of a mass slaughter in Rwanda. When asked to testify about their experiences, victims often become so overwhelmed that they are barely able to speak or are hijacked into such panic that they can't clearly articulate what happened to them. Their testimony is often dismissed as being too chaotic, confused, and fragmented to be credible. (p.246)

Having read through this much, the major thought I have had is: "how much have I been hurting myself by keeping my inner thoughts to myself?" Growing up in an immigrant Chinese family has heavily influenced how I share and express things about myself, and it's a hard pattern to break. I see myself slowly changing, but there's still a lot that I can learn that this book has been helping me understand. Perhaps it is time to take up journalling.
